What is Tongkat Ali?

Also called Pasak Bumi in Indonesia · Eurycoma longifolia Jack.

Tongkat Ali and Pasak Bumi are two names for one rainforest plant. This guide explains the names, where it grows, how the root is traditionally prepared, and how to read an extract label without being misled.

01

One plant, many names

Tongkat Ali means "Ali's walking stick" in Malay. In Indonesia the same plant is called Pasak Bumi, the "earth nail". Both names point to the same plant: Eurycoma longifolia.

In English it is also sold as Longjack. The botanical name is the reliable one, because several other plants and some mixed products are sold under similar common names. The botanical name Eurycoma longifolia on a label tells you which plant the root came from.

The plant belongs to the family Simaroubaceae, and the part traditionally used is the root.

02

Where it grows

It is a slender, slow-growing tree of the Southeast Asian rainforest understory, found across the region including Indonesia and Malaysia. It grows slowly, and the root takes years to mature, which is one reason quality material is not cheap.

Traditional useTraditionally used in Southeast Asian herbal practice to support general vitality, energy and well-being.

What to expect. Traditional herbs are part of a routine, not a quick fix. Individual experiences vary, and we make no promises about results.

03

How the root is traditionally prepared

Traditionally the root was cut, dried and simmered in water, and the water was drunk. This is why water is the traditional way to draw out the root, and why our extract uses water as its only solvent.

A modern extract does the same thing under controlled conditions: the root is extracted in water, the liquid is concentrated and dried, and the result is a powder that can be put into a capsule.

04

Reading an extract label

Many labels show a ratio such as 3:1, 100:1 or 200:1. The first number is how many kilograms of dried root were used to make one kilogram of finished extract. A higher number sounds stronger, but it needs a closer look.

  • In published laboratory work, boiling-water extraction of the root has been reported to give roughly 3% of the root's weight as extract. That makes a ratio of around 33:1 the practical ceiling for a plain water extract.
  • A label claiming 100:1 or 200:1 therefore needs an explanation, such as a concentrated fraction, added material, or a different meaning of the ratio. Ask the supplier what the ratio means and ask for the Certificate of Analysis.
  • A ratio alone does not tell you what is in the capsule. Look for the botanical name, the part used, the solvent, the carrier, and a batch Certificate of Analysis.

Our capsule is a 3:1 dry water extract, dried with corn starch. We state the ratio plainly because it is the honest description of what is inside.

Take care

Who should be careful

  • Not intended for use under 18, or by pregnant or nursing women.
  • Not for people with heart conditions, high blood pressure or kidney concerns.
  • Rare reports of liver problems have been published, mostly linked to high-dose use. If you have any liver concerns, speak to a healthcare professional first.
  • Speak to a healthcare professional before use if you have a medical condition or take medication.
WARNING: Keep out of reach of children. Do not exceed the stated dose.
